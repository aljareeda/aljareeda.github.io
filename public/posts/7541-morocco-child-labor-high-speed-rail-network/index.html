Morocco's Child Labor Fell 80% Since 1999 — But 101,000 Kids Still Work, State Council Warns

September 07, 2026

Sourced from the Kingdom of Morocco’s Official Gazette (Bulletin Officiel), issue №7541, dated September 7, 2026 (25 Rabi’ al-Awwal 1448). This digest translates and prioritizes the parts that actually matter to Moroccans abroad and outside observers — not the routine bureaucratic paperwork.

🧒 Child labor in Morocco has fallen 80% in 25 years — but 101,000 kids still work

Morocco’s Economic, Social and Environmental Council (CESE), a constitutionally-mandated state advisory body, has adopted a major opinion titled “For an Effective Public Policy to Eliminate Child Labor in Morocco,” and the numbers it lays out are the most concrete national child-labor accounting published in years.

The headline trend is genuinely positive: the number of working children aged 7 to 17 fell from roughly 517,000 in 1999 to about 101,000 in 2024 — from a rate that was several times higher down to just 1.3% of children in that age group. But the council stresses the phenomenon hasn’t disappeared, and it’s deeply uneven: children work at five times the rate in the countryside (2.5%) as in cities (0.5%), and the council links this gap directly to school dropout, family poverty, and the dominance of Morocco’s informal economy in rural areas.

To gauge public perception, the CESE ran a citizen consultation through its “Ashariك” digital platform. The gap between perception and the official statistics is striking: 78% of respondents believe child labor is “widespread” in Morocco (39% called it “very widespread”), and 60% believed it’s more common in cities — the opposite of what the official data shows. Respondents identified street work (27%) and involvement in illegal activities (22%) as the most visible forms, followed by family labor on farms or at home and work in small industrial/craft workshops (19% each). Nearly two-thirds (64%) said the work children do is constant, year-round labor rather than seasonal.

On causes, the public consultation pointed to poverty (20%), school dropout (15%), and parental unemployment (13%) as the top drivers. Asked what would help most, respondents prioritized direct support to economically vulnerable families (24%), building “second-chance school” bridges for children who left the system (20%), and stricter enforcement of the existing legal ban with real penalties (19%).

Legally, Morocco already bans work under age 15, and its Labor Code (Law 65.99) sets stiff conditions — mandatory medical checks, bans on night work and hazardous tasks — for 16-to-18-year-olds. A five-year transitional allowance that permitted limited work for 16-18-year-old domestic workers under strict safeguards expired on October 1, 2023. Morocco joined the international “Alliance 8.7” coalition in 2023 as a lead country on eliminating child labor, with a national roadmap targeting zero child labor under age 15, and the phase-out of hazardous work for 16-18-year-olds, by 2030. For context, the ILO estimates 138 million children aged 5-17 are in some form of work globally, 54 million of them in hazardous conditions, with Sub-Saharan Africa the hardest-hit region (87 million).

Opinion adopted by the CESE at its 182nd ordinary session, May 25, 2026; published in Bulletin Officiel №7541, September 7, 2026.

🚄 Morocco declares public utility for new Marrakech-Agadir and Chichaoua-Essaouira high-speed rail line

The government has formally declared “public utility” status — the legal step needed to start seizing and building on the land route — for a new high-speed rail line connecting Marrakech to Agadir, with a branch between Chichaoua and Essaouira. The line runs through the Marrakech-Safi and Souss-Massa regions, two of Morocco’s biggest tourism and agricultural hubs. The decree was signed by Head of Government Aziz Akhannouch and Transport Minister Abdessamad Kayouh.

Separately, in the same issue, the Transport Ministry authorized the seizure of land plots needed to widen the existing Kenitra-Marrakech high-speed line (the Al Boraq extension) at specific kilometer points in Bouskoura commune, Nouaceur province, part of the Casablanca-Settat region — a sign that work on Morocco’s flagship high-speed rail corridor, tied to preparations for the 2030 World Cup, is actively expanding on multiple fronts at once.

Marrakech-Agadir/Essaouira: Decree No. 2.26.605, September 1, 2026. Kenitra-Marrakech widening: Ministry of Transport and Logistics Decision No. 1540.26, July 15, 2026. Published in Bulletin Officiel №7541.

⚖️ Constitutional Court strips two lawmakers of their seats for switching political sides

Morocco’s Constitutional Court issued a batch of rulings declaring several parliamentary seats vacant after members resigned — but two cases went further. The court ruled that House of Representatives members Abdelghani Makhdad and Marouane Chabaatou had already forfeited their mandates automatically, by law, because each had quit the political party under whose banner they were elected before formally resigning from parliament. Under Article 61 of Morocco’s constitution, abandoning the political affiliation you ran under strips you of your seat outright — meaning their later resignation letters were legally moot; the court simply confirmed they’d already lost their status. The same batch of rulings also declared vacancies following straightforward resignations by other members of the House of Representatives and the House of Councillors, including a Draa-Tafilalet regional council representative.

The rulings are a reminder that Morocco enforces an anti-defection rule for elected officials — MPs can’t keep their seats after ditching the party list that got them elected, even if they’d rather resign quietly than be formally stripped.

Constitutional Court rulings issued September 2–3, 2026 (case numbers in the source scan are partially illegible). Published in Bulletin Officiel №7541.

🤝 Deaf and hard-of-hearing charity granted official “public utility” status

The “Lalla Asmae Foundation for the Deaf and Hard of Hearing,” based in Rabat, has been officially recognized by decree as an association of public utility — a status that unlocks tax and legal benefits, and lets it hold annual public fundraising drives without prior authorization (just 15 days’ advance notice to the government). The foundation is now authorized to hold property and assets worth up to roughly 138.4 million dirhams (about $13.8 million) to pursue its mission.

Decree published September 4, 2026 (22 Rabi’ al-Awwal 1448), in Bulletin Officiel №7541.
