Morocco Turns Its Port Authority Into a Company — and Locks In $1.2B a Year for Regions

August 17, 2026
Arabic Edition

Sourced from the Kingdom of Morocco’s Official Gazette (Bulletin Officiel), issue №7533, dated August 10, 2026 (26 Safar 1448). This digest translates and prioritizes the parts that actually matter to Moroccans abroad and outside observers — not the routine bureaucratic paperwork.

🚢 Ports of Morocco Becomes an Actual Company

The biggest structural change in this issue: Morocco’s National Ports Agency is being converted into a joint-stock company called “Moanئ Al-Maghrib” (Ports of Morocco SA), fully owned by the state. This isn’t a rebrand — it’s a legal shift from a public administrative body to a commercial company, meaning it can now run ports more like a business: signing concessions, managing tariffs, striking partnerships, and pursuing subsidiaries and joint ventures in port-related logistics, industry, and services.

The new company takes over essentially every port in the country except the Tangier–Med special economic zone and the Marchica lagoon development near Nador, which keep their own separate legal regimes. It also inherits the police/regulatory powers over port operations that used to sit with the state directly. For a country racing to upgrade transport and logistics capacity ahead of co-hosting the 2030 World Cup, turning the port operator into a nimbler corporate entity is a clear signal of where investment priority is headed — this is the same legal playbook Morocco already used for airports and highways.

Source: Law 34.25 amending Law 15.02, dated July 28, 2026.

💰 Regions Get a Guaranteed $1.2 Billion Floor, Starting 2027

Morocco just rewrote the rulebook for its 12 regions’ finances. Under a change to the organic law on regionalization, the state must now transfer a minimum of 12 billion dirhams a year (roughly $1.2 billion) to Morocco’s regions starting with the 2027 budget — on top of existing shares of corporate tax (5%), income tax (5%), and insurance-premium tax (20%) that regions already receive. Regions also gain new “own” powers over economic development — creating industrial zones, farm-produce marketing hubs, digital-development plans, and rural road building.

To execute projects, the old “Regional Agencies for Project Execution” in each region are being converted into joint-stock companies called “Regional Companies,” majority-owned by the region itself, giving regional governments a corporate vehicle to build things faster instead of routing everything through the central government. This is a genuine decentralization push — money and execution power both moving toward the 12 regions.

Source: Organic Law 031.26 amending Organic Law 111.14, dated July 24, 2026.

🏗️ Real Estate Overhaul: Faster Approvals, New Rules for Big Developments

If you own land in Morocco or are thinking about buying, this one matters. A new law rewrites how land subdivisions (lotissements), housing developments, and land partitions get approved — including a brand-new legal category called a “Major Public-Interest Development Operation” for large-scale projects over 400 hectares (smaller for state/public projects), designed for things like new urban districts and industrial hubs. The law also sets new deadlines: developers now have anywhere from 3 to 15 years (depending on project size, up to 15 years for developments over 400 hectares) to finish infrastructure work before their permit lapses, with a formal process for provisional and final handover of roads, water, and sewage networks to the local municipality.

Alongside this, the old system of “Urban Agencies” (Wikalat Hadariya) is being replaced by 12 new Regional Agencies for Urban Planning and Housing, one per region, giving regions direct control over zoning, land-use plans, and building permits instead of routing through a centralized urban agency. Casablanca’s urban agency is explicitly kept under its own older, separate law.

Source: Law 34.21 amending Law 25.90 (land subdivisions), and Law 64.23 (regional urban agencies), both dated July 28, 2026.

🏦 Bank Al-Maghrib Gets New Powers to Rescue Failing Banks

Morocco has built out a formal bank-resolution system — the legal machinery a country needs to step in and unwind or rescue a failing bank without a full-blown financial crisis or a bailout with taxpayer money. A new “Resolution Authority,” chaired by the head of the central bank (Bank Al-Maghrib) and including judges and finance-ministry officials, can now force a failing bank into forced sale, a “bridge bank,” or asset separation, and can wipe out shareholders and even certain bondholders before ordinary depositors take any hit. A yearly-updated list will flag which banks are “systemically important” (i.e., too important to fail) and subject to tighter scrutiny.

A Collective Fund for Deposit Guarantee and Resolution Financing is also created, fed by mandatory contributions from banks, to fund rescues without touching public money first. In plain terms: this is Morocco modernizing its financial safety net, aligning with the kind of “bail-in” bank-failure rules used in the EU and US since 2008–2013 — good news for anyone with savings or deposits in a Moroccan bank.

Source: Law 87.21 amending Law 103.12 (credit institutions) and Law 40.17 (Bank Al-Maghrib statute), dated July 28, 2026.

📈 Tax Revenue Beat Targets by Billions in 2024 — A Sign of Economic Strength

Morocco’s 2024 budget settlement law (essentially the country’s final, audited accounts for the year) shows the state took in 527.95 billion dirhams (about $53 billion) against 516.7 billion dirhams in spending, leaving an 11 billion dirham surplus. More striking: several major tax lines came in well above what was originally forecast — corporate tax collected 73.47 billion dirhams, about 20% above the 61.07 billion dirham target, and personal income tax brought in 60.12 billion dirhams versus a 52.88 billion dirham forecast. Value-added tax on imports alone brought in 58.46 billion dirhams.

For a country actively courting foreign investment, revenue coming in meaningfully ahead of plan — especially corporate tax — is a tangible signal that business activity and formal-sector growth outpaced government projections for the year.

Source: Law 04.26 (Settlement Law for the 2024 Finance Law), dated July 28, 2026.

🐾 A National Law Finally Regulates Stray Animals

Morocco has passed its first comprehensive national law on stray-animal welfare, requiring every municipality to run (or license private operators to run) proper stray-animal care centers — for capturing, vaccinating, sterilizing, and where possible releasing animals back to where they were found, rather than the ad hoc culling practices that have drawn international criticism in past years, including around World Cup-related events. The law criminalizes cruelty and killing of strays (2–6 months in prison plus fines up to 20,000 dirhams), while still allowing supervised humane euthanasia by a veterinarian in cases of irremediable suffering or public-health risk. Municipalities have up to two years to get compliant care systems running.

Source: Law 19.25 on the protection and care of stray animals, dated July 28, 2026.

🌍 National Research Center Formally Takes Over Earthquake Monitoring

Following the deadly September 2023 Al Haouz earthquake, Morocco has now formally written seismic monitoring into law: the National Center for Scientific Research (CNRST) is explicitly tasked with running the country’s national earthquake-detection network, alerting authorities when quakes strike, maintaining a public seismic database, and representing Morocco in international seismic-monitoring programs. The reform also gives the research center a broader mandate to run regional “technology transfer” hubs supporting startups and applied research around the country.

Source: Law 57.25 amending Law 80.00 on the National Center for Scientific Research, dated July 28, 2026.

📋 Also in this issue

A batch of smaller, mostly administrative reforms that won’t affect daily life for most readers but are worth a passing note:

  • Morocco’s notary (adoul) profession and judicial-expert profession both got full legal overhauls — new licensing exams, disciplinary bodies, and digital record-keeping requirements.
  • The National Council of the Order of Physicians (doctors’ professional body) was restructured, including new rules on elections and disciplinary panels.
  • Journalists’ professional status law was updated, including new protections around intellectual-property rights for their work.
  • The École Nationale d’Administration (formerly the “Higher National School of Administration”) was formally renamed and its training mandate expanded.
  • Tax incentives for companies that train and hire recent graduates were extended, retroactively covering hires made since January 2025.
  • A new National Statistics Council was created to oversee the reliability and independence of official economic data, alongside a reorganization of the High Commission for Planning.
  • The National Road Safety Agency’s law was tweaked to extend how long seconded civil servants can stay with the agency.
